Options, Futures & Risk Management

Mathematics of Finance builds the engine — measure theory, Brownian motion, Itô calculus, and the Black–Scholes formula falling out of a replicating portfolio. This course is what a trading floor does with that engine. It is the practitioner's companion: the instruments themselves (forwards, futures, swaps, exotic options), how a desk actually hedges a book of them, how a bank prices bonds and interest-rate risk, and how a risk manager answers the one question that matters most — how much could we lose?

This is a masters-level course covering the practitioner core of modern derivatives markets: forwards and futures markets, interest rates and the term structure, hedging, swaps, options trading strategies, binomial trees and other numerical methods, the Greeks in live use, the volatility smile, exotic options, interest-rate derivatives, credit derivatives, and — tying it all together — Value at Risk and the discipline of risk management. A final module looks at what happens when all of this goes wrong.

It assumes the Black–Scholes formula, the Greeks, and risk-neutral pricing from Mathematics of Finance, and occasionally leans on GARCH volatility models from Time Series Analysis.

The shape of the journey

Thirteen modules, moving through the subject the way a trading floor would: the instruments first, then how to hedge and trade them, then the numerical machinery that prices the hard ones, then risk management, and finally a look at what happens when it all goes wrong.

  • Module 2 — Interest rates & the term structure. Zero rates, bond pricing, duration, convexity.

Module 1 — Forwards, futures & the cost of carry

The simplest derivatives of all: a contract to trade an asset at a fixed price on a fixed future date. Their price is pinned down not by forecasting, but by a no-arbitrage argument — the cost of carry — that we'll use again and again through the rest of the course.

Module 2 — Interest rates & the term structure

Every discounted cash flow needs a rate to discount it with — but "the" interest rate is really a whole curve, one rate per maturity. We build that curve from bond prices, learn to read it as forward rates, and measure a bond's sensitivity to it.

Module 3 — Hedging with futures

A futures contract's real job, for most of the people who trade one, is not speculation but insurance: locking in tomorrow's price today. That insurance is rarely perfect — the mismatch is basis risk — and choosing the right hedge size is itself a small optimization problem.

Module 4 — Swaps

A swap exchanges one stream of cash flows for another — fixed rate for floating, one currency for another. It's the workhorse of the interest-rate derivatives market, built from nothing more exotic than a portfolio of forward contracts.

Module 5 — Options markets & trading strategies

Back to options — but now as a trader sees them: how the market is actually organized, what bounds arbitrage places on a price before any model is even needed, and the combinations (spreads, straddles, strangles) that turn simple calls and puts into a bet on almost anything — direction, range, or volatility itself.

Module 6 — Binomial trees & numerical methods

Black–Scholes has a closed form only because European options are simple enough. Most real options aren't — early exercise, path dependence, no formula in sight. The binomial tree (and its cousins, Monte Carlo and finite differences) prices anything, at the cost of doing the arithmetic on a computer instead of on paper.

Module 7 — The Greeks in practice

Mathematics of Finance derived the Greeks as sensitivities of a formula. Here they become a trader's steering wheel: delta-hedging a live book, why gamma makes that hedge slip between rebalances, and how a desk manages an entire portfolio of exposures at once.

Module 8 — The volatility smile

Plug real market option prices back into Black–Scholes and solve for the volatility that makes the formula match — and you'll get a different answer for every strike. The market, it turns out, doesn't quite believe the model it's pricing with.

Module 9 — Exotic options

Once you can price on a tree or by simulation, the payoff can be anything a client is willing to pay for: an option that switches off if the price touches a barrier, one whose payoff averages the price over time, one that looks back and picks the best moment.

Module 10 — Interest-rate derivatives

Options on interest rates need their own machinery, because the "underlying" — a whole yield curve — moves in ways a single stock price never does. We look at the standard market instruments, then at models built to describe the short rate itself.

Module 11 — Credit risk & credit derivatives

Every contract we've priced so far quietly assumed the other side pays up. Credit derivatives price the risk that they won't — turning "how likely is default?" into a tradeable number.

Module 12 — Value at Risk & risk management

Zoom out from any single position to an entire portfolio, and ask the question every risk manager is paid to answer: over the next day (or week, or month), how much could we plausibly lose? Value at Risk and its modern refinements try to give an honest number.

Module 13 — Derivatives disasters & lessons

Every tool in this course has, at some point, blown up spectacularly in someone's hands. A closing tour of the field's most famous catastrophes — and the very human lessons about leverage, model risk and hubris that risk managers are still relearning.
