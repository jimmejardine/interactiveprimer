Optical & Photonic Computing

Photonic computing is the art of making light do the work of electrons — carrying data down hair-thin waveguides at tens of terabits per second, multiplying matrices in the time it takes a laser pulse to cross a chip, and doing both for a fraction of the energy a copper wire would burn. Light is the world's best information carrier: it travels essentially for free, signals at different wavelengths pass through each other without colliding, and a lossless optical circuit is — by the physics itself — a unitary matrix in action. The catch, and half the drama of this subject, is that light is also the world's most stubborn switch: photons barely interact, so the transistor's easy trick of one signal throttling another is exactly what optics finds hardest.

This branch takes the subject to graduate depth: how light is guided, modulated and detected on a silicon chip; the Mach–Zehnder interferometer and the programmable meshes that turn interference into matrix arithmetic; photonic accelerators for neural networks and their honest energy accounting; Fourier optics, reservoir computers and Ising machines; the hard-nosed case of why all-optical digital logic lost to CMOS; the optical interconnects that are quietly rewiring datacenters today; photonic quantum computing from Hong–Ou–Mandel to fusion-based architectures; and the neuromorphic and programmable-photonics frontier.
