Coins and Notes

When you buy a comic or a bag of sweets, you pay with money. In Britain, money comes in two kinds: round metal coins that jingle in your pocket, and flat paper (or plastic) notes that fold up small.

Those are all the British coins: 1p, 2p, 5p, 10p, 20p, 50p, £1 and £2. The letter p is short for pence — a little bit of money. There are also notes, for bigger amounts:

£5 £10 £20

The number written on a coin or note always tells you what it is worth. Line them up from smallest to biggest and the numbers climb in order: 1p, 2p, 5p, 10p, 20p, 50p, £1, £2, £5, £10, £20 — each one is worth more than the one before it.

Coins are just like number bonds

You already know how to make a number bond — splitting a total into parts, like 2 + 2 + 1 = 5. Coins work exactly the same way: to find out how much money you have, you just add the coins together.

Here are three coins in a purse: a 2p, a 2p and a 1p. How much altogether?

2\text{p} + 2\text{p} + 1\text{p} = 5\text{p}

It is the very same sum you already know how to do — the coins just happen to be the things you are counting. And two 50p coins make a whole pound: 50\text{p} + 50\text{p} = \pounds 1.

Look closely and you'll spot that the 20p and 50p coins are not quite round — they have seven curvy straight sides. That's on purpose! It means you (or a machine, or someone who can't see well) can feel the difference between coins just by touching the edge. The £1 coin has twelve flat sides of its own, and the £2 coin is made of two different colours of metal, like a gold ring around a silver middle. Every British coin has its own special look, so you never have to guess.

Two coin traps that catch out new counters:

  • A bigger coin is not always worth more. The size of a coin and its value are not the same thing — you can only tell how much a coin is worth by reading the number on it, never by how big it looks.
  • More coins does not mean more money. Three 1p coins look like more because there are three of them, but 1\text{p} + 1\text{p} + 1\text{p} = 3\text{p}, and that is still less than a single 5p coin. Always add up the value, not the number of coins.

Soon you'll learn to write amounts properly with a pound sign and a decimal point, and work out change when you pay with more money than something costs — but first, get to know your coins really well. That's exactly what Money and Giving Change is all about.
