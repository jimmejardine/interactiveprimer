Mixed Geometry Problems

One figure, several theorems

Exam geometry rarely asks you to use one fact. The picture is a mash-up: a pair of parallels, an isosceles triangle sitting on them, a tangent off to the side, a smaller triangle nested inside a larger one. Each piece is a theorem you already know — angle chasing, similar triangles, Pythagoras, tangents — but the skill is spotting which one to fire next.

That is a different habit from the pages that taught each rule. There, the figure was built so the new theorem was the obvious move. Here the figure will not tell you. You have to try a mark, name a pair, and see what unlocks.

A working order

When a mixed figure looks noisy, walk the same short checklist every time:

  1. Mark what you are told — equal ticks, parallel arrows, a right-angle box, a given number. The picture is lying if you leave those invisible.
  2. Name the obvious pairs. Two ticks → isosceles base angles. Two arrows and a transversal → alternate or corresponding. A radius into a tangent → right angle.
  3. Close a triangle or a line once two of its angles are known — they add to 180^\circ.
  4. Only then reach for a length tool (a ratio, or Pythagoras). Lengths need an angle skeleton first.

Most mixed problems are three or four of those moves, not ten. The trap is starting in the middle.

Watch one mix

An isosceles triangle stands on one of two parallel lines, apex on the other. You are told the legs are equal. Nothing else. Three different theorems finish the figure:

Read it as a chain that changes family at every step: equal legs \Rightarrow isosceles gives two 50^\circ base angles \Rightarrow alternate interior copies them onto the top parallel \Rightarrow angles on a line leave 80^\circ at the apex. Same picture, three names.

A long chase is not automatically a mixed one. Vertically opposite, then angles on a line, then vertically opposite again is one crossing used three times. If every step cites the same family, you have not yet used the rest of the figure — look for the unused ticks, the unused parallel, or the unused right-angle box.
